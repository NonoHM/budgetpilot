# Why the brand looks like this

For contributors and designers who change the logo, its colours, or where the
accent appears in the app. It records what was decided, what was rejected, and
why, so a later change starts from the reasons rather than from taste.

## The mark is a folded ribbon

The symbol is a ribbon folded twice. It reads as a bank statement folded in
accordion, and the same zigzag draws a curve that rises and falls, the way a
balance moves through a month. Beside the name, its two right-hand points
suggest a B without spelling one.

It was chosen against three tests taken from the logo design literature:

- **Simple and distinctive at once.** Sagi Haviv, of Chermayeff & Geismar &
  Haviv, names this pair as the hard part of a mark. The ribbon is three
  shapes, and no other budgeting tool uses it.
- **Recognisable at 16 pixels.** David Airey's _Logo Design Love_ asks a mark
  to work small and to carry one idea. The browser tab is where this mark is
  seen most, so every candidate was judged there first, blurred and sharp.
- **Drawable from memory.** Haviv's doodle test: someone who saw the mark once
  should be able to sketch it. The final shape started as the owner's own
  sketch.

The following candidates were drawn and rejected:

- A plain monogram B, in several styles: legible, but generic.
- A B built from a chart (bars, an axis, candlesticks): the letter or the chart
  was lost at 16 pixels.
- Abstract shapes such as a split coin, an artificial horizon, a gauge or a
  flight path: each resembled a familiar interface icon or another brand.
- The ribbon with a dot added to complete the B: at tab size the dot vanished
  or read as a speck of dirt, and it added a second idea.

## The accent is lime, and only lime

The back face of the ribbon is lime-400, `#a3e635`. Three other accents were
tried and set aside:

- **Amber** is already the app's warning colour. It appears in the warning
  banners of 14 of the 98 components measured, and a logo in the same colour
  would blur the line between the brand and an alert.
- **Rose** reads as a loss in finance, the opposite of what a budgeting tool
  wants to signal.
- **Teal, sky and emerald** are the colours most money and banking products
  already use, so they distinguish nothing.

Lime appeared in none of the app's files when it was chosen, and it stays clear
of emerald, which the app uses for money coming in.

Wise uses a similar bright green. Ours is yellower and more saturated, and it
sits on a black and white identity rather than on Wise's pairing of green with
forest green. No colour mark registered by Wise was found, but that search was
not a registry check, and the project has had no legal review.

## The tile stays dark everywhere

The app icon is always the ink tile, on a light or a dark tab. A favicon can
read the operating system's colour scheme but not the colour of the tab it sits
in, so a transparent icon can disappear on a themed browser. iOS and Android
require an opaque icon anyway. One fixed shape everywhere is also easier to
recognise. The transparent variants exist for documents, where the background
is known.

## Lime never carries white text

Lime is a light colour, so white text on it fails the contrast WCAG 2.2 asks
for. The ratio is 1.51 to 1, against a minimum of 4.5 to 1 for text of this
size. Darkening the lime until white passes, at lime-700, turns it olive and
loses the colour of the logo. Lime therefore carries dark text or no text at
all, and lime text never appears on a light surface.
